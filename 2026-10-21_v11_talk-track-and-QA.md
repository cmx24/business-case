## 0. How to deliver it

### 0.1 Seven rules for an executive business case

1. **Answer first, then the support.** Open each slide with its one-line point. If they stop you after the first sentence, they already have the point.
2. **One number per sentence.** Say the number, say its unit, say what it means, and pause. Don't read tables aloud; point at them and say what they mean.
3. **Name the decision owner every time.** The panel is testing judgment, and that includes knowing who decides. The CFO decides, the CEO arbitrates, the Board approves, and the functions own their technical calls.
4. **Say what you don't know, once per section, with a plan.** Every weight, level and date in the deck is a proposal. Say so at the start of each section, not on every line. For genuine gaps, use section 0.8.
5. **Signpost the deliverables.** Say "Deliverable 1 of 4" through "Deliverable 4 of 4". An interrupted panel can then find its way back.
6. **Concede fast, defend slowly.** If a challenge is right, say so in the first sentence. If it is wrong, take the reasoning step by step. Panels trust people who concede quickly; it makes the defenses more credible.
7. **Close on the decision.** Thanking the panel is fine, but the last substantive words are the ask.

### 0.2 Pace, pauses and breath

**Baseline is 130 words a minute, which sounds calm.** Nerves push most people to 160 or more. If you finish a slide ahead of its budget, you are rushing; slow down, don't add words.

| Speed | Words per minute | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| **Slow** | about 110 | Numbers, definitions, walk-aways, the ask |
| **Steady** | about 130 | Default. Explanation and process |
| **Lift** | about 150 | Signposts, recaps, bridges the panel already expects |

**Never lift on a number.**

**Pauses**

- `/` is a beat (1 second) at a clause boundary. It's also your breath.
- `//` is a full pause (2-3 seconds). Use it after a number, after the headline of a slide, and before the ask. Count silently: "one-thousand-one, one-thousand-two."
- Silence after a number reads as confidence. Filling it reads as doubt.

**How to say a number.** Say it, then its unit, then its meaning. Pause. Example: "Thirty-five million. / About two tenths of a point of efficiency ratio. //" Round when you speak. Never say "eight hundred and six point two million".

**Start of every slide.** Click on a beat, drop your pitch slightly, and give the panel one second to read the title before you speak. They read first and listen second.

**Sentences.** Keep each under about 20 spoken words, with one idea. If a sentence needs a breath in the middle, split it.

### 0.3 Tone arc

The same voice for 24 minutes becomes wallpaper. Shift the register by section, and keep every shift small.

| Section | Slides | Tone | What it sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | 1-2 | Warm, calm, composed | A colleague describing a situation, not a candidate selling |
| Planning and money | 3-7 | Precise, matter-of-fact | Plain numbers, no drama, no apology for the small ones |
| Board and executives | 8-10 | Conversational, respectful | You are discussing people you respect; never sound superior |
| HQ | 11-13 | Measured, careful, slightly slower | Irreversible decisions get unhurried language |
| Leadership and close | 14-15 | Personal, then firm | Real stories, then a clear ask |

**Throughout:** confident without certainty. "Our recommendation is" and "the data points to" are strong; "obviously" and "clearly" are weak. Sound like a person who will change their mind when the evidence changes, because that is the pitch.

### 0.4 Emphasis: how and which words

**How**

1. **One stress per clause, at most.** Stress the new information, not the verb.
2. **Slow down before the stressed word and pause after it.** Pausing does more than volume.
3. **Drop your volume instead of raising it.** A quieter line at the key moment pulls the room in.
4. **Contrast pairs** carry emphasis for free: "not a brake, / a lever"; "evidence, / not the number"; "informs, / doesn't decide".
5. **Triads** land: "propose, implement, assess"; "rules public, conversations private, sign-off joint".
6. **Repeat a landing phrase word for word.** Don't re-word it each time. The panel remembers what it hears twice.

**The landing phrases.** One per section. Say it exactly like this.

| 2 | "**Ownership**." | Slide 2 | One word, a full pause after it. |
| 3 | "Start small. **Grow on evidence.**" | Slide 5 | Lower volume, steady pace. |
| 4 | "**Nobody hears a flat no.**" | Slide 7 | Slow, after a beat. The close of the money section. |
| 5 | "**Decisions, not read-outs.**" | Slide 9 | Contrast pair; stress both. |

### 0.5 Voice: "we", not "I"

The role is a function that serves the CFO and the markets, not a personal platform.

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "I will drive alignment" | "The team brings the options; the CFO decides" |
| "I built a framework" | "We propose a framework, to agree with the CFO" |
| "My approach is…" | "The way this function works is…" |
| "I'll make sure the GMs…" | "The GMs own their numbers; we make them comparable" |
| "I believe strongly that…" | "The data points to…" / "Our recommendation is…" |
| "We decided…" (about CFO calls) | "The CFO decides…" |

Keep "I" for three moments only: your personal stories, "where I'd push back", and honest gaps ("I don't know that yet"). Everywhere else, use "we", "the team" or "the function".

### 0.6 Words and habits to avoid

**Words on the slides you should not read out.** The deck uses some phrasing that sounds fine on a page and wrong aloud.

| On the slide | Say instead | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "CEO arbitrage" (slide 2) | "the CEO arbitrates" / "the CEO decides" | "Arbitrage" means something else to a finance panel |
| "resolves executive differences" (slide 8) | "brings options to the executives" | You have no authority to resolve; the CFO and CEO do |
| "synergy" / "REAL synergy" (slide 10) | "alignment" | A cliché, and it sounds like consulting |
| "dissent but commit" (slide 10) | "disagree and commit" | The idiom the panel knows |
| "Leveraging" / "Leverages" AI (slides 3, 8) | "using" | The verb sounds like consulting |
| "T-28", "T-16" (slides 8, 9, 20) | "twenty-eight days out", "sixteen days out" | Jargon, and v10 already says so |
| "CoE", "PMO", "SMEs" | "the hub", "the project office", "the experts" | Say what you mean |
| "CoS & CG" | "the team" or "we" | Nobody talks like that |
| "People, people, people" (slide 10) | Read as: "the people side" | Cute on a slide, too casual aloud |

**Habits**

- **Fillers.** "So basically", "what I want to highlight", "as you can see", "to be honest" (implies the rest wasn't), "obviously", "clearly".
- **Reading the slide.** The panel can read. Say what the slide doesn't.
- **Turning to the screen.** Glance, then return to the panel. Point with an open hand.
- **Apologizing for density.** "I know this is a busy slide" tells them it is.
- **Stacked hedges.** "Sort of", "kind of", "maybe", "I think" before a fact.
- **Upspeak on numbers.** A number that ends in a rising tone sounds like a question.
- **"Great question".** It buys time and everyone hears it. Use it once, or never. A one-second pause does the same job.
- **Answering the question you wish you'd been asked.**
- **Criticizing a previous employer** or naming a competitor's weakness.
- **Speaking for executives.** Don't attribute views to David Vélez or Rob Livingston that you have not seen them state publicly.
- **Picking a side** in the capital disagreement, or in any dispute between named executives.

**Content traps in the talk**

- Don't say the **$60M is approved**. $25M is in the plan; the rest is conditional.
- Don't call the **$25M "the budget"**. It is the cap.
- Don't call the Q1-to-Q2 efficiency-ratio move (17.6% to 19.5%) a **trend**. Management guides to about 20% for FY26.
- Don't present **weights, levels or dates** as final. They are proposals.
- Don't **name a city** for the HQ, and don't claim an HQ saving.
- Don't imply you would **sign** or sit as an officer.
- Don't say **AI decides** anything. AI supports; people validate and decide.

### 0.8 Intellectual humility: using "I don't know" on purpose

**Why it fits Nu.** Nu's Code of Conduct (October 2023 version, section "We Act Like Owners, Not Renters", as summarized in search results; I could not open the PDF) describes relationships built on humility, respect, transparency and responsibility, with no room for ego, and with learning from mistakes encouraged. A Nu careers post also lists being honest about your growth areas as a quality it values. Treat these as Nu's stated aspirations, not proof of practice, and confirm the wording before quoting. None of this is a statement by Vélez or Livingston.

**Why it works here.** A panel asked to approve a $25M cap, a Board calendar and an HQ method is looking for someone who knows where the edge of their knowledge is. Someone who is right about the method and honest about the gaps is more trustworthy than someone who is certain about everything. "I don't know" is strong when it is **bounded**: you name the gap precisely, say why it matters, say how and when you'll close it, and offer an interim position.

**The four-part bounded unknown**

1. **Name the gap precisely.** "I don't know the current planning cycle time."
2. **Say why it matters.** "It sets whether forty days is a stretch or a giveaway."
3. **Say how and when you'll close it.** "We'd measure it in the first two weeks, from the stand-ups and the Q1 release."
4. **Offer an interim position.** "Until then, I'd treat forty days as a target to agree, not a promise."

Total length: 20-30 seconds. Say it level, make eye contact, do not apologize.

**Eight phrasings, in Nu's register**

| # | Phrase | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "I don't know that yet, and I'd rather say so than guess. Here's how we'd find out, and by when." | A fact you don't have |
| 2 | "That's a hypothesis, not a finding. What would change my mind is…" | A claim you inferred |
| 3 | "That's Tax's call, not mine. What I'd do is make sure the question reaches them at gate one, in writing." | An expert's domain |
| 4 | "You've found a gap. I'd close it this way…" | A fair challenge you can't rebut |
| 5 | "Let me think about that rather than answer it badly. Can I come back to you in writing by Friday?" | A genuinely hard question |
| 6 | "My instinct is X. I hold it loosely, because I haven't seen [the data]." | A judgment call |
| 7 | "From the outside, the evidence points to X. I don't have Nu's internal view." | Nu internals |
| 8 | "I may be wrong on that one. The number I'd watch is…" | A prediction |

**Budget: use it 3-5 times in the session, no more.** Used too often it becomes a pattern and sounds like a dodge. Q&A answers tagged `[BU]` in section 4 are the natural places.

**Where not to use it**

- **The numbers on the slides.** They are sourced and you reviewed them. "I don't know" about your own slide reads as unprepared.
- **Your method, your recommendation or the ask.** Those are the things you came to own.
- **Two answers in a row.** Pair an unknown with a firm answer next.
- **A question you could answer with five seconds of thought.** Think first.
- **To dodge.** If the real reason is that you'd rather not say, say that.

**After the session.** If you promised a written follow-up, send it within 24 hours. That turns "I don't know" into proof of ownership.

### 0.9 Five beats for every answer

1. **Pause.** Two seconds. Look at the person who asked. Don't start on the first word of their question.
2. **Answer first.** One sentence: yes, no, or "it depends on X". No wind-up.
3. **Anchor.** One number or one slide. "Slide 7."
4. **Boundary.** What you don't know, or who owns the call. One sentence, only if true.
5. **Stop.** Don't fill the silence. At most, bridge with: "Does that answer it?"

**Target:** under 45 seconds. If it needs more, say "That's a longer answer, can I give you the short version and the slide?"

**Handling question types**

| Type | Example | Move |
|---|---|---|
| **Clarifying** | "What do you mean by readiness?" | Define it in one sentence with an example. |
| **Challenge** | "Isn't this just bureaucracy?" | Concede the real risk first, then the safeguard. |
| **Loaded premise** | "Since the CEO is wrong about X…" | Restate neutrally: "The two views are A and B." Don't accept the premise. |
| **False choice** | "Speed or control?" | "The design aims for both; the trade-off is on speed, and slide 5 says so." |
| **Hypothetical** | "What if the Board rejects it?" | Give the first move and the owner. |
| **Personal** | "Tell me about a time…" | Use a prepared story, section 0.10. |
| **Out of scope** | "What's Nu's FX hedging policy?" | "That's outside this case, and I don't have the facts. Here's where I'd look." |
| **Multi-part** | "How, when and who?" | "Three parts. Taking them in order." Number them. |
| **Hostile** | A sharp or dismissive tone | Stay level, lower your pace, thank them once, restate a neutral version, answer that. |
| **Silent panelist** | Someone hasn't spoken | At a natural break: "Is there something you'd want to push on?" |

**Park it.** If an interruption would derail the slide: "Can I come back to that in Q&A?" Write it down where they can see you do it.

### 0.11 Room and body

- **Eyes.** Look at the panel, not at the slide. Look at the CFO when you say "decide". Look at the person who asked while they ask.
- **Hands.** Point at a number with an open hand. Rest them when you're not pointing. Don't hold the clicker like a pen.
- **Feet.** Stand still on the ask and on the walk-aways. Move between sections, not during a sentence.
- **Water.** Take a sip at the two planned pauses for questions (after slides 7 and 10).
- **Clicking.** Click on a beat, not mid-word.
- **Watch.** Put it face up on the table. The checkpoints are in section 3.
- **If you are on video.** Camera at eye level, slow down about 10%, and hold pauses a beat longer because of latency.

### Slide 1: Chief of Staff to the CFO & Global Corporate Governance

**Time** 1.5 min · **Pace** steady, warm · **Tone** calm, composed

**Points to make**

- Promise the rigor *and* the speed. That is the whole tension of the role.

**Script**

> Good morning, / and thank you for the time. //
>
> Let me start with where we are. **Sixty days in**, / three things have landed at once. //
>
> **One.** Sixty million dollars of growth asks / against a **twenty-five million** cap. //
>
> **Two.** A Board meeting in **four weeks**, / with two executives who see capital differently. //
>
> **Three.** A new regional headquarters that cuts across Legal, Tax, Compliance, People and Workplace, / and nobody who owns all of it. //
>
> Three different problems. / **One thread**: finance and governance as **growth levers**, not a brake. / The job is to add the rigor that scale needs, / **without losing the speed** that got Nu here. //

**Stress:** sixty days in · twenty-five million · four weeks · growth levers · without losing the speed
**Pause on:** after "cap", after "four weeks", before "One thread", after "three decisions"
