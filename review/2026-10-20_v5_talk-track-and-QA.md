# Talk track and Q&A: Chief of Staff to the CFO & Global Corporate Governance (deck v5)

Deck: `deck/CFO_CoS_deck_v5.pptx`, 22 slides. Slides 1-14 are presented; slide 15 is the backup divider, and backups A-G
(slides 16-22) are for Q&A only. Format: 30 minutes presenting, then 30 minutes of Q&A. This version replaces
`review/2026-10-20_talk-track-and-QA.md`, which was written for the 19-slide v4.

How to read this file:
- Lines starting with `>` are what you say. Everything else is for you.
- Numbers are written as you would say them.
- Sentences are short, with one number per breath. If a sentence feels long out loud, split it. Don't add to it.
- `[your example, about 40 words: ...]` marks a story only you can tell. The timing counts each one at its stated length.
  None has been written for you on purpose (board item B16): an invented story is the biggest risk in an executive interview.
- `[Pause.]` and `[Switch to slide 3.]` are stage directions and are not counted.

## 1. Talk track, slide by slide

### Slide 1: Chief of Staff to the CFO: planning, the Board and the new HQ

> Good morning, and thank you for the time.
>
> Sixty days in, three problems landed on my desk at once. Sixty million dollars of growth asks against a twenty-five million cap. A Board meeting in four weeks, with two executives pulling opposite ways on capital. And a regional HQ that cuts across legal, tax and real estate, where I have no line authority.
>
> I triaged them by clock. The Board date is fixed, so it goes first. The budget sets the precedent for every cycle after it. The HQ gets chartered now and paced by gates.
>
> I will ask you for three decisions, and I will flag each deliverable as I reach it.

**Delivery.** These are your first 60 seconds. Learn them word for word. Speak slower than feels natural and look at
each panel member once. Pause after "at once" and again after "by clock". Don't read the slide.

### Slide 2: Three decisions today: the $25M plan, the Board calendar and the HQ charter

> Here are the three decisions, with the number behind each.
>
> Deliverable 1 is planning. Twenty-five million, funded in two releases. Fifteen million on the first of January, for cases already vetted. Ten million at Q1 close, if the numbers hold. No flat cuts.
>
> Deliverable 2 is the Board. Both executives' positions go through one capital test. The plan locks sixteen days before the meeting. The book goes out seven days before.
>
> Deliverable 3 is the HQ. Four gates in one hundred and twenty days: tax, grant terms, officers and the lease. Each one has an owner and an exit test.
>
> Deliverable 4 is how I work. I sit in the numbers with the GMs. The CFO gets the trade-offs and makes the call.
>
> So the ask has three parts. Approve the twenty-five million FY27 cap in two releases. Approve the Board calendar. Approve the HQ charter.
>
> You should hear one more thing now, not later. Up to fifteen million more is held in reserve, outside the plan. The CFO decides on it, and Risk can block it.
>
> Why a cap, and not a reshuffle of the existing base? The cap is the CFO's. It is set for public-market expectations and margin discipline, and it sits inside the FY26 guide of about twenty percent. Inside the cap, money moves every quarter.
>
> I report back in ninety days.

**Delivery.** Point at each tile as you name it. Say "the CFO decides" while looking at the CFO. Bringing up the reserve
yourself is deliberate: if the panel finds it later, it looks hidden.

### Slide 4: Underwrite budgets like credit lines: the precedent matters more than the $35M

> Deliverable 1 of 4: planning.
>
> First, scale. Thirty-five million is small for Nu. Revenue runs at about five point nine billion a quarter, so the gap is about fifteen basis points of annualized revenue. It is about one point one percent of annual opex, and about two tenths of a point on the efficiency ratio.
>
> So I won't oversell the money. What matters is the precedent, for three reasons.
>
> One: the constraint is the efficiency ratio. It went from seventeen point six percent in Q1 to nineteen point five in Q2. Management named real-estate and marketing timing, and international expansion. Unvetted central spend pushes that number the wrong way.
>
> Two: cost to serve is one dollar a month, up from eighty cents a year earlier. ARPAC is seventeen dollars ten. That gap stays wide only if central costs grow slower than customers.
>
> Three: this is the first cycle planned centrally. It decides whether GMs bring real cases next year, or pad their asks.
>
> The principle comes from Nu itself. The 20-F describes low-and-grow: customers start on small limits and earn bigger ones on behavior. I want budgets to work the same way. Start small. Grow when four indicators hold: activation, thirty-day retention, CAC against plan, and unit cost against plan.
>
> For the CFO, every release reads the same four numbers. For the GMs, there is a funded start and a published way to earn more. That is smart efficiency, run by owners.

**Delivery.** Signpost clearly ("Deliverable 1 of 4"). Say the scale numbers quickly and plainly: you are shrinking the
number, not selling it. Slow down on "low-and-grow". This idea carries the whole deck.

### Slide 5: Staged release beats flat cuts and core-first

> I looked at three ways to close the gap.
>
> An equal cut gives every unit forty-two percent of its ask. It is fast, but it scores fifty-seven point eight. It teaches GMs to pad next year, and it starves launches below viable scale.
>
> Core markets first scores sixty-three point eight. It is honest, but new bets get nothing to earn.
>
> Staged release, based on business-case readiness, scores ninety-one point eight. It wins on growth return, ownership and agility. It loses only on speed.
>
> The scores and weights are my judgment, and the slide says so. So I tested them. Move any single weight by ten points and option 3 still comes first. Its smallest lead is twenty-four points. That is backup C.

**Delivery.** Brisk. Say "my judgment" before anyone else can. If someone challenges the scores here, answer in one
sentence (Q23) and keep going.

### Slide 9: When a senior GM pushes back: four moves, in the same order every time

> When a senior GM pushes back, I make the same four moves, in the same order, every time.
>
> First, I talk to them privately. I confirm the strategic goal before any budget line comes up. Nobody gets corrected in front of the CFO.
>
> Second, I move to the evidence: from "I need twenty-five million" to "what would prove this works?" The published rule does the arguing, not me.
>
> Third, I offer a conditional yes instead of a flat no. That means a funded start, like the four and a half million for new bets, and a reserved claim on the second release once the indicators hold.
>
> Fourth, we present the plan together at the lock. The CEO's office is briefed in advance and sees the same rule.
>
> Why does this work without line authority? People accept a decision they argued against when the criteria were public before it was made.
>
> [your example, about 40 words: one time a senior leader asked you for more than you could give; what you offered instead; the result, with one number.]

**Delivery.** Warm and practical. This is about people, not finance, so drop your pace and soften your voice. The example
is the payoff: tell it as a story (situation, what you did, the result) and stop.

## 4. Q&A, one question at a time

Each entry has the same parts:
- **Why they ask:** what the question is really testing.
- **Position:** the one idea to land.
- **Say:** a spoken answer of under 60 seconds.
- **Tone:** how to deliver it.
- **Show:** the slide to put on screen. Name it at the end of the answer, not the start.
- **If they push** (some entries): the follow-up answer.

General rules: start with the answer, not with "Great question". If you don't know, say what you would do to find out
and by when. Keep the backup slide ready, but don't put it on screen unless they want detail.

### A. The ten most likely questions

**Q1. Cost to serve rose to $1.0. Why, and doesn't that undermine your cap?**
- **Why they ask:** To see whether you read the numbers or just quote them.
- **Position:** Be honest about what isn't public, then turn it into the case for the cap.
- **Say:** "It went from eighty cents to a dollar a month in a year. The public filings don't break that down, so I won't
  guess at the drivers; I'd get the breakdown from FP&A this week. What Q2 did name is international expansion, plus
  real-estate and marketing timing, behind the move in the efficiency ratio. That's the case for the cap, not against it.
  A dollar against seventeen-ten of ARPAC stays a wide margin only if central costs grow slower than customers. Thirty-five
  million is about one point one percent of opex. Small, but it sets the habit for every cycle after this one."
- **Tone:** Calm and factual. Say what you don't know first; it makes the rest more credible.
- **Show:** Slide 4.

**Q2. Your reserve spends above $25M. How is that inside guidance?**
- **Why they ask:** It's the obvious weak point: a cap with a door in it.
- **Position:** The reserve is zero unless profit beats plan, and it is sized to the guide, so the guide holds either way.
- **Say:** "Fair challenge. The twenty-five million is the plan. The fifteen is a reserve, and it isn't in the plan. It
  exists only if first-half profit beats plan, and it's sized so the efficiency ratio stays inside the roughly twenty
  percent guide. If profit doesn't beat plan, it's zero. If credit costs or NPL run above plan, it stays shut, and Risk can
  block it. No GM can trigger it. IR decides how it's disclosed: as upside spend, not a change to guidance. So the guide
  holds either way."
- **Tone:** Unhurried. Start with "Fair challenge" and mean it.
- **Show:** Slide 6, then slide 13.
- **If they push ("then why have it at all?"):** "Because without it, a strong H1 turns into a mid-year scramble for
  money. With it, the rule for that conversation is written before anyone needs it."

**Q3. The Board topic is capital allocation. Where's the capital in your deck?**
- **Why they ask:** To test whether you saw that the real dispute is about equity, not a $25M opex line.
- **Position:** Deliverable 2 opens with it, and A4 says what happens if the dispute is all about capital.
- **Say:** "It opens Deliverable 2. There are four uses of equity: Brazil deepening, Mexico as a bank since August with
  four point two billion to 2030, the US charter that has to be capitalized by about the end of January, and the buyback
  of up to one billion. I'd rank them on after-tax return on the extra equity each one needs, against that market's cost of
  equity, with entity headroom under a downside. The buyback is the bar. The CFO recommends, the CEO decides, and the Board
  approves at this meeting, so Investor Day on the eighth of December rests on an approved plan."
