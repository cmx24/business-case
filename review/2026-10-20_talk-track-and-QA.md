## 1. Per-slide talk track (slides 1-19)

### Slide 1: Title: three problems, four deliverables, one ask

Good morning, and thank you for the time. Sixty days in, this page is what I found: sixty million dollars of growth asks against a twenty-five million cost cap, a Board meeting four weeks out with two executives holding opposite views on where the money should go, and a regional HQ deal that touches legal, tax and real estate across more than one jurisdiction, with no line authority for me over any of those teams. I will take you through four deliverables that answer those three problems, and I will end with one ask. Every Nu figure I use is from the public Q2'26 filings, and every assumption of mine is on slide 3, where you can test it.

### Slide 2: Executive summary and the ask

Here is the whole case in three decisions, each with a number attached. First, planning: the twenty-five million is FY27's growth-opex envelope, and I fund it through two business-case gates, fifteen million at the start of FY27 and ten million at Q1 close on the Q1 indicators, with no flat haircuts anywhere in the plan. So the ask today is to approve three things: the twenty-five million FY27 cap in two gates, together with a CFO- and IR-gated valve of up to fifteen million that is not in plan and that I will show you the rule for; the T minus sixteen and T minus seven Board calendar; and the HQ project charter. ### Slide 4: Why the cap matters at Nu's scale

I want to be honest about materiality. The thirty-five million gap is about fifteen basis points of annualized revenue on a base of roughly twenty-three billion, and under one percent of about four point six billion of opex, so I am not going to oversell it to a CFO who reports results at that scale. Three things make it matter anyway. The efficiency ratio came in at nineteen point five percent in Q2, up from seventeen point six in Q1, with international expansion named as a driver, and management expects about twenty percent for the year; unvetted overhead is exactly what moves that number the wrong way. Cost to serve is one dollar a month against seventeen dollars ten of ARPAC, up from eighty cents a year earlier, and that margin stays wide only if central costs scale slower than customers. And this is the first budget cycle planned centrally, so how the thirty-five million is closed decides whether GMs bring real cases next year or pad their asks.

(169 words)

### Slide 5: The planning principle: underwrite budgets like credit lines

This is the one idea the rest of the deck hangs on. Nu's 20-F describes low-and-grow underwriting: customers start on small limits and earn larger ones as repayment data comes in, so exposure grows only behind evidence. I apply the same logic to budgets, which replaces one annual negotiation with quarterly evidence; it is Nu's own test-and-learn discipline, applied to Nu's own money. For the CFO, every release decision reads the same four indicators for every unit: activation, thirty-day retention, CAC against plan and unit cost against plan, with reallocation across units every quarter rather than once a year. For the GMs, it means a funded Q1 start and a published rule for unlocking the next stage, so the conversation moves from positions to indicators.

(125 words)

### Slide 6: Three ways to close $35M

I looked at three ways to close the gap before choosing. An equal percentage cut gives every unit forty-two percent of its ask; it scores ninety-five on speed and fifty-seven point eight overall, because it teaches GMs to pad next year and starves launches below viable scale. Core markets first scores sixty-three point eight; it is honest, but it gives the new bets nothing to earn. Business-case readiness with staged release scores ninety-one point eight, winning on growth return, ownership and agility, and losing only on speed. The scores and weights are my judgment, labeled as assumption A9, so the test I care about is robustness: move any single weight by ten points and option 3 still finishes first every time.

### Slide 7: The $60M bridge

This slide answers the whole sixty million; nothing is cut, and every piece has a path. Eight million of country asks are met by reusing the shared global code base at near-zero marginal cost: these are requests that duplicate features the platform already ships. That is my estimate, assumption A5, and the rule is simple: Engineering confirms it in week one, or whatever they cannot confirm moves into the case track. Twelve million are early ideas without a business case today; they enter a sixty-day track where my team builds the case with the GM, and they come back to gate 2 with evidence rather than a number. That leaves twenty-five million funded in two gates, which is the cap, and the arithmetic closes: eight plus twelve plus fifteen plus twenty-five is the sixty million on the left of the chart. I want to say plainly what this does for the two executives. Neither gets a no: the core-market advocate gets the largest release first, and the new-bets advocate gets a protected start and a published route to more. ### Slide 8: The operating model and the release rule

The structure is hub-and-spoke. A central hub of six to eight people takes the annual cycle and the single model in-house from the business units, reads Nu's data platform rather than spreadsheets, and is measured on three things: cycle time under forty days, forecast accuracy, and one hundred percent of spend with an approved case. Finance partners sit inside the country and vertical squads with a solid line to the hub and a dotted line to the GM; they build the cases with the GMs and own the indicator read-outs at each gate. Inside the cap there are two gates on the fiscal calendar: fifteen million at FY27 start on the first of January to the vetted cases, ten and a half million to core markets and four and a half to new bets, then ten million at Q1 close on the thirty-first of March to the units whose Q1 indicators hold. Above the cap sits the A6 valve: up to fifteen million in H2, only from H1 profit already in the bank, sized to stay inside the efficiency guide, with CFO and IR sign-off, and disclosed as upside spend rather than a change to guidance.

(196 words)

### Slide 9: The cadence: four meetings

Four meetings, each with an owner and an output, and nothing else goes on the calendar. Weekly squad clinics, where finance partners model unit economics with the GMs before anything reaches formal review; the output is a business case in the standard template, co-signed by the GM. Bi-weekly one-to-ones, where I stress-test each GM's assumptions privately and design the staged option before the CFO sees it, so there are no surprises at the T minus sixteen lock. A weekly forty-five-minute HQ pod that I chair, with Legal, Tax, Workplace and People around one decision log and forty-eight-hour escalation to the CFO. And the quarterly tranche release, where the CFO decides on my recommendation and the release memo goes to the GMs the same day. Decision roles are explicit: partners recommend, GMs give input, I assemble the pack, the CFO decides, the hub performs.

(143 words)

### Slide 10: Settling the two views

The fix for two opposing executives is a shared yardstick, not a compromise number. Core markets, Brazil, Mexico and Colombia, get seventy percent of the gate-1 release, ten and a half million, because mature cohorts reach about thirty-one dollars of monthly ARPAC at eight years against seventeen dollars ten for the group, at thirty-three percent ROE, so proven markets protect the year. New bets, the new verticals and markets, get thirty percent, four and a half million, and are graded on activation, retention and unit cost, because a new bet judged on mature-market profit in its first quarter always loses. Over the full year that is sixteen and a half million inside the cap plus up to five under rule A6 for core markets, and eight and a half million plus up to ten for new bets, which gives the new bets first claim on up to fourteen million across gate 2 and the valve, if it opens. Both can sign the FY27 opex lock because each is measured on what it can actually show. I write the one-page options memo, the CFO recommends, the CEO decides, and the chair gets the pre-read; this is opex, and equity for Mexico and the US is the Board's own decision, two slides on.

Finally, the GM and I present the staged plan together at the T minus sixteen lock, and there is no back channel to the CEO because there is nothing left to appeal. ### Slide 12: The capital question the Board will actually ask

Here is the slide I would most want the panel to remember, because the Board's larger decision is equity by entity, not a twenty-five million opex line. Four uses of equity are on the table at once: deepening in Brazil; Nu Mexico, authorized as a bank in July 2026 and operating as one since August, with a four point two billion dollar plan to 2030; the US de novo charter, which has to be capitalized within twelve months of the OCC approval of the twenty-ninth of January 2026; and the buyback of up to one billion dollars announced in June. The yardstick is return on allocated capital: rank each use by after-tax return on incremental allocated equity against that market's cost of equity, with capital headroom by entity under a downside case. Two consequences follow. A use that beats its cost of equity creates value even if it dilutes the thirty-three percent group ROE, so ROE dilution alone is not a reason to say no; and the buyback is the bar every growth use has to clear, because the Board can always return the money. On the sequence: the CFO recommends the ranking, the CEO decides, and the Board approves at this meeting, so the Investor Day narrative on the eighth of December rests on an approved plan rather than the other way round. My job is to produce one page per use with the same four lines, return, capital, headroom and risk, and to put the chair's pre-read in front of him at T minus nine. The cost-of-equity figures are to be set with Treasury; I have not guessed them. If you ask me where the next dollar should go, my answer is wherever return on allocated capital beats the local cost of equity by the widest margin after the downside, with the US sized to its roughly one hundred basis point envelope until its credit models mature.

### Slide 14: Statutory governance across new entities

For a bank, the entity question is a licensing question first. A new entity is opened when a license or tax substance requires it, never for scale alone, and the officers and signing follow the stage. In a market test there is no local entity, the market is served from an existing licensed entity or a partner bank, and there is central signing only, which prevents an accidental permanent establishment. At licensed scale-up, the US charter or a new vertical, a dedicated entity gets its own capital plan and headroom, fit-and-proper directors, two central officers plus one local leader, dual signature above a set limit, and D&O bound before activation. For the core hubs, Mexico as a bank since August 2026, Colombia and wherever the HQ lands, there is Audit and Risk Committee oversight, dual signature above limits set by category, director liability insurance, and an annual review of powers of attorney. The signing limits are proposals, and local CFOs are already in place in Mexico and Colombia.

(168 words)

### The ten expected questions

**Q1. Cost to serve is $1.0. Why did it rise, and why does that support the cap rather than undermine it?**

So the cap is not a judgment that the asks are bad; it is a rule that every dollar inside the $25M FY27 envelope has to show up with a case and four indicators, which is what keeps the $1.0 from drifting.
