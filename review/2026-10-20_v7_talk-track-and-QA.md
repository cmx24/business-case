# Talk track and Q&A: Chief of Staff to the CFO & Global Corporate Governance (deck v7)

Deck: `deck/CFO_CoS_deck_v7.pptx`, 26 slides. Slides 1-15 are presented; slide 16 is the backup divider, and backups A-J
(slides 17-26) are for Q&A only. Format: 30 minutes presenting, then 30 minutes of Q&A. v7 gives every deliverable 2-3 slides
with an assessment, a recommendation, the data behind it and its risks (D1: slides 4-6; D2: 7-9; D3: 10-12; D4: 13-14), from
the reframe in `review/2026-10-20_fable-reframe.md` as assessed and adapted. This file replaces the v4-v6 talk tracks.

How to read this file:
- Lines starting with `>` are what you say. Everything else is for you.
- Numbers are written as you would say them.
- Sentences are short, with one number per breath. If a sentence feels long out loud, split it. Don't add to it.
- `[your example, about 40 words: ...]` marks a story only you can tell. The timing counts each one at its stated length.
  None has been written for you on purpose (board item B16): an invented story is the biggest risk in an executive interview.
- `[Pause.]` and `[Switch to slide 3.]` are stage directions and are not counted.

## 1. Talk track, slide by slide

### Slide 1: Chief of Staff to the CFO: planning, the Board and the new HQ

> Good morning, and thank you for the time.
>
> Sixty days in, three problems landed on my desk at once. Sixty million dollars of growth asks against a twenty-five million cap. A Board meeting in four weeks, with two executives pulling opposite ways on capital. And a regional HQ that cuts across legal, tax, compliance and real estate, where I have no line authority.
>
> I triaged them by clock. The Board date is fixed, so it goes first. The budget sets the precedent for every cycle after it. The HQ gets chartered now and paced by gates.

**Delivery.** Your first 60 seconds, with slide 2's opening line. Learn them word for word. Speak slower than feels
natural and look at each panel member once. Pause after "at once" and again after "by clock". Don't read the slide.

### Slide 2: Three decisions today: the $25M plan, the Board calendar and the HQ charter

> I will ask you for three decisions, and I will flag each deliverable as I reach it.
>
> Deliverable 1 is planning. Twenty-five million, funded in two releases. Fifteen million on the first of January, for cases already vetted. Ten million at Q1 close, if the numbers hold. No flat cuts.
>
> Deliverable 2 is the Board. Both executives' positions go through one capital test. The plan locks sixteen days before the meeting. The book goes out seven days before.
>
> Deliverable 3 is the HQ. Four gates in one hundred and twenty days, each with an owner, an exit test and a walk-away. Licensed executives sign; I run the process.
>
> Deliverable 4 is how I work. I sit in the numbers with the GMs. The CFO gets the trade-offs and makes the call.
>
> So the ask has three parts: the twenty-five million FY27 cap in two releases, the Board calendar, and the HQ charter.
>
> You should hear one more thing now, not later. Up to fifteen million more is held in reserve, outside the plan. The CFO decides on it, and Risk can block it.
>
> Why a cap, and not a reshuffle of the existing base? The cap is the CFO's, set for public-market expectations and margin discipline, inside the FY26 guide of about twenty percent. I report back in ninety days.

**Delivery.** Point at each tile as you name it. Say "the CFO decides" while looking at the CFO. Bringing up the reserve
yourself is deliberate: if the panel finds it later, it looks hidden.

### Slide 3: What I know, and the assumptions that could break the plan

> This is what I know, and what I am assuming.
>
> The first row is fact from the Q2 release: one hundred thirty-eight point nine million customers, an efficiency ratio of nineteen point five percent, and thirty-three percent ROE. The second row is management's own guidance: about twenty percent for FY26, and the US entry within about one hundred basis points of it.
>
> The rest are mine, and two matter most. A4: I assume the executives disagree about the growth-opex envelope, not about regulatory capital by entity. If I am wrong, the capital slide carries the decision, which is why Deliverable 2 starts there. And A12: any net new cost for the hub and the HQ is small, and it sits inside the twenty-five million. If it is material, the second release shrinks.
>
> I am confirming both now, along with the Board calendar and who owns the planning model today.

**Delivery.** Even, factual pace. Slow down on A4 and A12 and say them as risks you own, not disclaimers. This is the
slide you come back to at the end.

### Slide 4: Staged release beats flat cuts: the precedent matters more than the $35M

> Deliverable 1 of 4: planning.
>
> First, what is broken. The asks arrive as one sixty-million-dollar number. There is no shared template, no shared data, and the cap is set top-down. So the argument is about the number, never the evidence.
>
> Second, scale. Thirty-five million is small for Nu. Revenue runs at about five point nine billion a quarter, so the gap is about fifteen basis points of annualized revenue. It is about two tenths of a point on the efficiency ratio. So I won't oversell the money.
>
> What matters is the ratio and the habit. The efficiency ratio went from seventeen point six percent in Q1 to nineteen point five in Q2, with real-estate and marketing timing and international expansion named. Unvetted central spend pushes it the wrong way. And this first central cycle decides whether GMs bring real cases next year, or pad their asks.
>
> I looked at three ways to close the gap. An equal cut gives every unit forty-two percent of its ask. It scores fifty-seven point eight, and it teaches GMs to pad. Core markets first scores sixty-three point eight, but new bets get nothing to earn. Staged release on business-case readiness scores ninety-one point eight. The scores are my judgment, so I tested them: move any single weight by ten points, and it still wins.
>
> The principle comes from Nu itself. The 20-F describes low-and-grow: small limits, raised on behavior. Budgets get the same test. Start small, and grow when four indicators hold: activation, thirty-day retention, CAC and unit cost.

**Delivery.** Signpost clearly ("Deliverable 1 of 4"). Say the diagnosis plainly; it is the assessment the panel is
listening for. Say the scale numbers quickly: you are shrinking the number, not selling it. Point at the score column, not
each cell. Slow down on "low-and-grow"; this idea carries Deliverable 1.

### Slide 5: Every dollar of the $60M gets an answer: $25M funded in two releases, $15M held in reserve

> Every dollar of the sixty million gets an answer. Read the chart from the top.
>
> Eight million is met on the shared platform. These asks duplicate features the global code base already ships. That is my estimate, and Engineering confirms it. Whatever they cannot confirm moves into the case track, and the cap does not grow.
>
> Twelve million are ideas without a business case yet. They enter a sixty-day track with my team, and come back for the second release with evidence, not a number.
>
> Fifteen million is the H2 reserve, and it is not in the plan. It opens only from H1 profit above plan, inside the roughly twenty percent guide, with NPL ninety-plus on plan. The CFO decides. Risk can block it. No GM can trigger it.
>
> That leaves twenty-five million funded. Fifteen on the first of January: ten and a half to core markets, four and a half to new bets. Ten at Q1 close, to the units whose indicators hold. The money is dated the thirty-first of March; the decision comes on Q1 data in early April.
>
> Neither executive hears a no. Core markets get the larger share first. New bets get a protected start and a published route to more. And the CFO gets something new: every first-release dollar carries an approved case.

**Delivery.** The most important slide in Deliverable 1. Point at each bar as you speak. Don't add the numbers up out
loud; the chart does that. Slow down on the reserve conditions, and look at the CFO on "the CFO decides".

### Slide 6: A lean hub and three meetings bring planning in-house

> How does this run? With a planning hub of four to six people.
>
> It owns one planning model and reads Nu's data platform directly. Its KPIs are cycle time under forty days, forecast accuracy, and one hundred percent of spend with an approved case. Finance partners sit in each market: solid line to the hub, dotted line to the GM.
>
> Two AI agents do the consolidation, which is why four to six people is enough. A PMO agent reads status where the work already happens, so nobody fills in the status templates everyone hates. A review agent checks every case against the template and the data. People still own every number.
>
> There are three standing meetings, and nothing else goes on the calendar: weekly squad clinics, GM one-to-ones every two weeks, and a quarterly release review where the CFO decides.
>
> The brief asks for capacity allocation, so every case states the people and engineering capacity it needs. Engineering confirms it at the lock. A release never funds work the platform cannot staff.
>
> Three ways this fails. GMs game the indicators: finance partners own the read-out. The platform estimate slips: the cap does not grow. The hub becomes a cost the GMs resent: any net new cost sits inside the cap and is shown first, and if cycle time slips, I simplify instead of hiring.
>
> [Pause.] That closes the money. Is there anything on the plan you'd like to test now?

**Delivery.** Say the AI lines in a matter-of-fact voice, as plumbing rather than a pitch; "the status templates
everyone hates" can get a smile. Point at each risk card as you name it. This is the first planned pause for questions.

### Slide 7: The capital dispute gets one ranking: return on the equity each use needs

> Deliverable 2 of 4: the C-suite and the Board. I start with capital, because that is what the Board will ask about.
>
> Four uses of equity are on the table at once. The first is Brazil deepening.
>
> The second is Nu Mexico: authorized as a bank on the tenth of July and operating since the sixth of August, with four point two billion dollars planned to 2030.
>
> The third is the US charter. The OCC gave conditional approval on the twenty-ninth of January. Capital has to be in within twelve months, so by about the end of January 2027. That is roughly ten weeks after this Board.
>
> The fourth is the buyback: up to one billion dollars, announced in June.
>
> I would rank all four on one test: the after-tax return on the extra equity each use needs, against that market's cost of equity. And I would check headroom by entity under a downside case.
>
> A use that beats its cost of equity creates value, even if it dilutes the thirty-three percent group ROE. And the buyback is the bar, because the Board can always hand the money back.
>
> The CFO recommends, the CEO decides, and the Board approves at this meeting, the last before Investor Day on the eighth of December. I produce one page per use. The cost of equity comes from Treasury. I have not guessed it.
>
> Then the opex half of the same answer. Mature cohorts earn about thirty-one dollars a month at eight years, against seventeen dollars ten for the group. So core markets take seventy percent of the first release, and new bets take thirty. A new bet judged on mature-market profit in its first quarter always loses.

**Delivery.** The most senior content in the deck: your most measured pace and lowest pitch. Say "I have not guessed it"
plainly, not as an apology. Pause before "A new bet judged on mature-market profit".

### Slide 8: When a senior GM pushes back: four moves, in the same order every time

> When a senior GM pushes back, I make the same four moves, in the same order, every time.
>
> First, I talk to them privately. I confirm the goal before any budget line comes up. Nobody gets corrected in front of the CFO.
>
> Second, I move to the evidence: from "I need twenty-five million" to "what would prove this works?" The published rule does the arguing, not me.
>
> Third, I offer a conditional yes instead of a flat no: a funded start, and a reserved claim on the second release once the indicators hold.
>
> Fourth, we present the plan together at the lock. The CEO's office is briefed in advance and sees the same rule.
>
> With the two MTeam members, I run the same four moves separately, next Tuesday, before they meet at the lock.
>
> Why does this work without line authority? People accept a decision they argued against when the criteria were public before it was made.
>
> [your example, about 40 words: one time a senior leader asked you for more than you could give; what you offered instead; the result, with one number.]

**Delivery.** Warm and practical. This is about people, not finance, so drop your pace and soften your voice. The example
is the payoff: tell it as a story (situation, what you did, the result) and stop.

### Slide 9: The Board book goes out 7 days before the meeting, after a lock at 16 days

> Why is the Board book late? Because nothing is locked: no outline, no standard data tables, and units building rival decks. So the fix starts today, with the Corporate Secretary.
>
> I am assuming the Board sits on Tuesday the seventeenth of November. Q3 results come out on the twelfth.
>
> In week one, the outline and the tables are locked. In week two, I hold one-to-ones with each executive, and then the capital memo settles the ranking and the seventy-thirty split with the CEO and the CFO, sixteen days out. Week three has one pen: inputs freeze at ten days, chairs are briefed at nine, and the book is on the portal at seven. In week four, directors' questions are answered in writing, so the meeting is spent on the capital plan.
>
> Two things cannot slip: the capital plan, and the statutory items for new entities. My rule there is to open an entity only when a license or tax substance requires it.
>
> Three risks. If the executives still disagree at the lock, both positions go in the memo, the CEO decides, and the dissent is recorded. If materials are still late, the locked outline decides what goes to the appendix. And the book holds Q3 results the market hasn't seen, so it lives on the portal only. The review agent drafts on Nu-approved tools, and a named owner signs each answer.
>
> [Pause.] That is the Board. Any questions before the HQ?

**Delivery.** Open with the diagnosis; it answers "why is it late" before anyone asks. Then crisp and operational, like a
run-sheet. Say "days out" instead of "T-minus" out loud. Second planned pause for questions.

### Slide 10: The HQ is a governance project before it is a real-estate deal

> Deliverable 3 of 4: the HQ. It is a governance project before it is a real-estate deal.
>
> Here is the assessment. I am negotiating a regional hub and campus across Legal, Tax, Compliance, People and Workplace, in more than one jurisdiction, with no line over any of them. And the brief doesn't name the jurisdiction.
>
> Three things can go wrong before a lease even exists. Tax residence moves, or a taxable presence appears. The incentive is worth less than it looks once Pillar Two applies. In 2026, only about nine percent of payroll and seven percent of tangible assets come out of the top-up base. Or an officer signs before they are approved and insured.
>
> Two kinds of agreement are on the table. Local regulatory agreements: the incentive, any tax ruling, and permits. And campus agreements: the lease, fit-out, utilities and zoning.
>
> So how would I choose the site? Four hard tests first, pass or fail: no license we lack, no move in tax residence, officers who pass fit-and-proper, and real payroll and assets. Then five weighted criteria, with talent first. I start where Nu already has licensed substance and a local CFO, or where it is building. Two sites go to term sheet, and the CEO and CFO pick.

**Delivery.** Signpost "Deliverable 3 of 4". Steady and concrete. Linger on the Pillar Two line, the one technical point a
CFO will remember. Don't name a city: the method is the answer.

### Slide 11: Four gates in 120 days, each with an owner, an exit test and a walk-away

> Four gates in one hundred and twenty days, each with an owner, an exit test, and a line I will not cross.
>
> Gate 1, at day thirty, is tax and residence. Tax owns it, with Compliance. The exit test is an opinion for both sites that residence doesn't move and the modeled rate holds under Pillar Two. The walk-away is any site where residence moves, whatever the incentive.
>
> Gate 2, at day sixty, is the regulatory and grant terms. Legal owns it. I would negotiate proportional scaling: eighty-five percent of the headcount keeps eighty-five percent of the grant, with twenty-four months of grace. We drop one site here. The walk-away is a cliff-edge clawback, or a headcount commitment above our plan.
>
> Gate 3, at day ninety, is officers and signing, owned by Legal and People. Officers come from licensed executives, and D&O is bound. The fit-and-proper files started back at gate 1, because regulator approval is the critical path.
>
> Gate 4, at day one hundred twenty, is the lease. Workplace owns it. The cost is phased, because Q2 showed how real-estate timing moves the efficiency ratio. The walk-away is a lease with no break option, or cost committed before the incentive is signed.
>
> How I negotiate: Legal leads the room, I set the mandate with the CFO, and nothing is signed outside a gate.

**Delivery.** Read across each row: gate, owner, exit, walk-away. Use your hands to count the gates. Say the walk-aways
firmly; they are the risk management.

### Slide 12: Licensed executives sign; I run the pod, and the CFO breaks ties

> Who signs? Licensed executives, after gate 3. Until then, signing stays central, which also avoids an accidental taxable presence. After it, dual signature above set limits, and powers of attorney limited in scope and time.
>
> I am not a statutory officer, and I hold no power of attorney. Officers carry personal liability for the entity's filings and conduct, so D&O names the new entity before it activates. And if the hub entity is regulated, it needs its own capital plan, which puts it on the Board's statutory agenda.
>
> How do I lead five functions I don't manage? One weekly forty-five-minute pod: Legal, Tax, Compliance, People and Workplace, each with one lead who can commit their function. Functions recommend. I assemble the gate pack and recommend. The CFO decides the gates, and the CEO decides the site.
>
> When functions disagree, their words go in the decision log. They get forty-eight hours to find a joint view. If they can't, both options go to the CFO, with mine. Legal and Compliance can veto on legality. Nobody vetoes on price.
>
> The risks I watch are tax residence, officer approval running late, and dependence on me. That last one is covered by a named deputy and a decision log the CFO can read without me.

**Delivery.** Left column first, then right. "Nobody vetoes on price" is the line people remember; pause before it. Say
"dependence on me" lightly, as self-awareness.

## 4. Q&A, one question at a time

Each entry has the same parts:
- **Why they ask:** what the question is really testing.
- **Position:** the one idea to land.
- **Say:** a spoken answer of under 60 seconds.
- **Tone:** how to deliver it.
- **Show:** the slide to put on screen. Name it at the end of the answer, not the start.
- **If they push** (some entries): the follow-up answer.

General rules: start with the answer, not with "Great question". If you don't know, say what you would do to find out
and by when. Keep the backup slide ready, but don't put it on screen unless they want detail.

### A. The ten most likely questions

**Q1. Cost to serve rose to $1.0. Why, and doesn't that undermine your cap?**
- **Why they ask:** To see whether you read the numbers or just quote them.
- **Position:** Be honest about what isn't public, then turn it into the case for the cap.
- **Say:** "It went from eighty cents to a dollar a month in a year. The public filings don't break that down, so I won't
  guess at the drivers; I'd get the breakdown from FP&A this week. What Q2 did name is international expansion, plus
  real-estate and marketing timing, behind the move in the efficiency ratio. That's the case for the cap, not against it.
  A dollar against seventeen-ten of ARPAC stays a wide margin only if central costs grow slower than customers. Thirty-five
  million is about one point one percent of opex. Small, but it sets the habit for every cycle after this one."
- **Tone:** Calm and factual. Say what you don't know first; it makes the rest more credible.
- **Show:** Slide 4.

**Q2. Your reserve spends above $25M. How is that inside guidance?**
- **Why they ask:** It's the obvious weak point: a cap with a door in it.
- **Position:** The reserve is zero unless profit beats plan, and it is sized to the guide, so the guide holds either way.
- **Say:** "Fair challenge. The twenty-five million is the plan. The fifteen is a reserve, and it isn't in the plan. It
  exists only if first-half profit beats plan, and it's sized so the efficiency ratio stays inside the roughly twenty
  percent guide. If profit doesn't beat plan, it's zero. If credit costs or NPL run above plan, it stays shut, and Risk can
  block it. No GM can trigger it. IR decides how it's disclosed: as upside spend, not a change to guidance. So the guide
  holds either way."
- **Tone:** Unhurried. Start with "Fair challenge" and mean it.
- **Show:** Slide 5, then backup I (slide 25).
- **If they push ("then why have it at all?"):** "Because without it, a strong H1 turns into a mid-year scramble for
  money. With it, the rule for that conversation is written before anyone needs it."
