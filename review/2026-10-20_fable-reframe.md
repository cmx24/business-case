Prepared 2026-10-20. Inputs: the case brief (`deck/Nu_case_scope.pdf`), deck v6 (`deck/CFO_CoS_deck_v6.pptx`, 14 presented slides, backups A-H),
the v6 talk track and Q&A (`review/2026-10-20_v6_talk-track-and-QA.md`), the three-agent review, the pit wall decisions B1-B24,
`PITWALL/REGRESSION.md` and the Nubank business-case skill. Nothing was edited. This file is an assessment and a specification; the
build is a separate step.

The brief's own words are the scoring sheet. Where I disagree with an earlier decision I say so and give the trade-off.
Every figure below is already verified in section 5 of the v6 talk track, or is labeled as a proposal. No new Nu data, benchmarks,
savings figures, incentive values or site names are introduced.

## 0. Bottom line

- The candidate's read is right: Deliverable 3 is the thinnest answer in the deck, and the brief gives it the same weight as the other
  three. One slide carries four gates and one footer; it says nothing about how a site is chosen, what is negotiated, where the
  walk-away points are, who may sign, what a statutory officer actually carries, or what happens when Legal and Tax disagree.
  A panel that includes the General Counsel's or Tax's view will find the bottom of it in two questions.
- Deliverables 1 and 2 are strong on recommendation and data and weak on diagnosis and risk. Deliverable 4 answers the brief's
  question on one slide but shows no "balance": nothing says what the candidate does with their own hands in each of the three
  problems, and the derailer and stories are still empty slots (B16, B22).
- Recommendation: 15 presented slides, 24.5 minutes at 130 words per minute, every deliverable with 2-3 slides that each carry
  assessment, recommendation, data-grounded rationale and risks with mitigations. That breaks B19 and the `max_presented=14`
  line in REGRESSION.md by one slide. The trade is explicit in section 2.1, and I recommend taking it: the 14-slide limit was a
  means (protect D3 and D4 from interruptions), not an end, and the new storyline is shorter in minutes than v6.
- Depth is balanced, not shifted to D3. Each deliverable is sized by what the brief asks of it: D1 and D2 keep three slides each
  (the brief's longest deliverable texts); D3 goes from one slide (2.4 min) to three (5.5 min) at the same density as D1 and D2,

  end with two slides of real substance (3.6 min). The time comes from the opening and close (3.1 and 1.4 minutes against 4.0 and
  2.6 today), from merging the principle and options slides in D1, and from moving the scenario table to backup.

Per-deliverable budget: opening 3.1 min (3 slides); D1 5.4 (3); D2 5.5 (3); D3 5.5 (3); D4 3.6 (2); close 1.4 (1). Total 24.5.

## 1. Scope and challenge assessment, per pillar

### 1.1 Pillar 1: in-housing global corporate planning; $60M of asks against a $25M cap

**What the panel is really testing.** Whether the candidate can build a planning function that the CFO trusts and the GMs will use,
in the first cycle it exists. The $35M is a prop. The brief says so twice: "without stifling business agility or creating friction with
senior GMs", and the deliverable asks for a "framework ... capacity allocation, and managing the $35M gap". Three things are being
scored: the design of the function (people, model, cadence, decision rights), the allocation rule (how a dollar earns its way in), and
the handling of the gap without a flat cut.

**The hard part.** Not the arithmetic. It is (a) the cap covers "all central and regional operations", so the planning hub, the HQ's
run-cost and every GM ask compete inside the same $25M; (b) "capacity allocation" is people and engineering capacity, not only money,
and a country ask that needs platform engineering competes with every other ask for the same engineers; (c) the first cycle sets the
habit: whatever is rewarded this year is what GMs bring next year.

**What an excellent answer contains.** A diagnosis of why today's decentralized process produces a $60M lump (no shared template, no
shared data, a cap set top-down, so the argument is about the number not the evidence); a rule that gives every dollar an answer; an
operating model with its own KPIs and decision rights; a statement of what sits inside the cap; and the two or three ways it fails,
each with an owner.

**How v6 scores: strong on recommendation and data, adequate on operating model, thin on diagnosis and risk.**
- Strong: slide 6 (every dollar gets an answer, two releases, the reserve with "the CFO decides, Risk can block"); slide 4's scale
  framing on the CFO's own metric (about 0.2 points of efficiency ratio); the low-and-grow principle from the 20-F; option scoring
  with a sensitivity in backup C.
- Adequate: slide 7's hub (4-6 people, KPIs, three meetings, roles) is concrete, but "capacity allocation" appears nowhere; the
  $8M platform item is the only place engineering capacity is implied.
- Thin: there is no assessment of what is broken today, only a principle; the cap's coverage of central costs (hub, HQ run-cost) is
  never stated, and a CFO will ask whether the hub eats the GMs' money; D1's risks live on slide 13, six slides later, where the
  listener has lost the thread. The second release "at Q1 close, 31 Mar" assumes Q1 data is closed on 31 Mar; with a 40-day cycle
  time it will be an April decision, which is fine but should be said.

### 1.2 Pillar 2: C-suite alignment and the Board rhythm

**What the panel is really testing.** Whether the candidate can get two senior people who disagree to a single recommendation
before the Board sees it, without authority over either; and whether they can run a Board process that ships on time with late
inputs. The brief adds "statutory governance across expanding legal entities" as a key Board topic, which most candidates skip.

**The hard part.** The two MTeam members' disagreement is about capital, and the candidate has no standing to settle it. The only
tools are a published test, private conversations, and the CEO's decision rights. "Gain buy-in without direct line authority" is the
explicit scoring line. The second hard part is sequencing: Q3 results are due 12 Nov, the Board is assumed on 17 Nov, so the book at
T-7 carries results the market has not seen.

**What an excellent answer contains.** One capital test both executives can accept before they know the result; a named decision
path (CFO recommends, CEO decides, Board approves); a calendar that locks the plan before the book; a diagnosis of why materials are
late; a rule for what gets cut; the statutory agenda for new entities; and what happens if the two do not converge.

**How v6 scores: strong on the capital test and the calendar, adequate on pushback, thin on diagnosis, non-convergence and the
statutory topic.**
- Strong: slide 8 (four uses of equity with dates, return on allocated capital against cost of equity, the buyback as the bar,
  "cost of equity comes from Treasury"); slide 10's T-28 to T-0 calendar with Investor Day as the fixed point.
- Adequate: slide 9's four moves are good and human, but they are written for a GM asking for budget, and the brief's harder case is
  two peers of the CFO disagreeing on capital. The talk track handles it (T-21 one-to-ones, lock at T-16), the slide does not.
- Thin: no line on why the book is late (no owner, no standard tables, rival decks: the fix is on the slide, the diagnosis is not);
  no stated outcome if the executives still disagree at T-16 (the answer is simple: both positions in the memo, the CEO decides,
  dissent recorded, the Board sees one recommendation and the alternative considered); the statutory topic is one sentence plus
  backup B, for a topic the brief names as a Board agenda item.

### 1.3 Pillar 3: the HQ, regional hub and campus negotiation

**What the panel is really testing.** Whether the candidate can lead a project that is not finance, across Legal, Tax, Compliance,
People and Workplace, in several jurisdictions, with the CEO and CFO as sponsors and nobody reporting to them. The brief's words are
precise: "negotiating local regulatory and campus infrastructure agreements", "statutory officer requirements across multiple
jurisdictions", "acting as an interim leader across non-finance verticals", and the deliverable asks for "milestone gates, statutory
officer implications, and cross-functional leadership". Every one of those phrases should be visible on a slide.

**The hard part.** Four things at once. (1) The jurisdiction is not given, so the candidate must show how they would choose, not
pretend to know. (2) A regional hub for a Cayman-incorporated group with a Brazilian operating base can move tax residence or create a
taxable presence if people sign and decide in the wrong place; the project can destroy value before a lease is signed. (3) Statutory
officers carry personal liability and need regulator approval where the entity is regulated; who sits in those seats, and when they
may sign, is a governance decision the Board will ask about. (4) The functions will disagree (Tax wants substance, Workplace wants the
cheapest lease, Legal wants no clawback, People wants relocation certainty), and an interim leader has to make that productive.

**What an excellent answer contains.** A diagnosis ("this is a governance project before a real-estate deal"); a site assessment
framework with hard tests before weighted criteria; what is in a "local regulatory agreement" and a "campus infrastructure
agreement" and who owns each term; a gated roadmap with owners, exit tests and walk-away points; the statutory officer model (who
signs, liability, fit-and-proper, D&O, powers of attorney, taxable presence); decision rights for the pod, including what happens when
functions disagree; the top risks with mitigations; and the honest line that no savings figure exists until a site is chosen.

**How v6 scores: thin.** Slide 11 has four gates, each with an owner and an exit test, one Pillar Two fact, and a footer that says the
jurisdiction is open. That is a good skeleton and the right instinct. Missing on the slide: how the site is chosen; what is negotiated
and where the walk-away points are; the officer model (it is in backup B and Q9, not on screen); Compliance (the brief names it; the
pod has Legal, Tax, People, Workplace); decision rights and the disagreement path; the risk register as more than a list of five nouns;
the interim-leader model beyond "a weekly pod and a decision log". A reader of the slide alone could not say how the candidate would
run the negotiation.

### 1.4 Pillar 3 / Deliverable 4: leadership style and operating philosophy

**What the panel is really testing.** The brief says "briefly", and asks one specific question: how the candidate balances high-level
strategic influence with hands-on technical FP&A and governance rigor in a fast-paced, matrixed culture. They want to see both halves
and the person behind them.

**The hard part.** Showing the balance rather than asserting it. "I build the model and read the clauses myself" is an assertion;
"here is what I do with my own hands in each of the three problems, and here is where I stop and let the CFO decide" is evidence.

**How v6 scores: adequate.** Slide 12's three rules and the "push back / leave alone" strip are good and in the candidate's voice.
The three-company opening (Citi, Itaú BBA, Visa) is in the talk and is the candidate's own. Missing: the balance itself, shown per
problem; the team the candidate builds (Nu's "strong and diverse teams" value; R3 flagged it); the derailer (B22) and the three
examples (B16), which only the candidate can write.

### 1.5 Opening and close

Slides 1-3 take 4.0 minutes and slides 13-14 take 2.6. The ask is stated on 2 and 14 (fine); the stop rules on 13 and 14 (once is
enough); the reserve on 2, 6 and 13 (define it once on 5, name it on the close). The scenario table on slide 13 is a good CFO artifact
but it is D1's risk slide in disguise; once each deliverable carries its own risks, it belongs in backup.

## 2. Reframed storyline

### 2.1 The slide-count decision

B19 and REGRESSION.md fix the presented count at 14. The reason was R3's: with 19 slides and 27.9 minutes, the first interruption
pushed D3 and D4 off the end. That reason argues for a time limit, not a slide limit. The reframe below is 15 slides in 24.5 minutes,
shorter than v6's 25.4, and it puts D3 at 5.5 minutes instead of 2.4, level with D1 and D2. The trade-off: one more slide to flip,
and the "8-10 core slides" practice in the skill's Mode A is stretched further. I think the trade is clearly right, because the brief
weights the four deliverables equally and D3 at one slide fails the candidate's own standard. If the pit wall keeps 14, the cut is
slide 14 (D4's second slide): fold its "team" and "watch-out" lines into slide 13 and accept that the balance is shown in the talk,
not on screen. I would not cut D3 back below three.

**How each deliverable's size was set.** By the brief's own wording, not by which one is weakest today. D1 asks for three things
(a framework for in-housing, capacity allocation, the $35M gap): three slides. D2 asks for three (GM pushback, buy-in without line
authority, pre-Board routines) plus the statutory topic: three slides. D3 asks for three (milestone gates, statutory officer
implications, cross-functional leadership): three slides, one per item, with the site framework and negotiation stance kept to one
block each and the full menu (scorecard weights, agreement checklist, five-row risk register) in backup J. D4 says "briefly": two
slides, the rules and the balance shown per problem.

Change REGRESSION.md: `max_presented=15`; add a timing check (`talk_minutes<=26` at 130 words per minute, from `timing.py`);
`max_slides=26` (15 presented, divider, 10 backups).
