## 0. How to deliver it

### Best practice for executive business cases

- **Answer first, then the support.** Open each slide with its one-line point, then give the support. If they stop you after the first sentence, they already have the point.
- **One number per sentence.** Say the number, pause, and move on. Don't read tables aloud; point at them and say what they mean.
- **Name the decision owner every time.** The panel is testing judgment, and that includes knowing who decides. In this deck the CFO decides, the CEO arbitrates, the Board approves, and the functions own their technical calls.
- **Say what you don't know.** "This is a proposal, to agree with the CFO" is stronger than false precision. Every weight, level and date in the deck is labeled as a proposal; say so once per section, not on every line.
- **Signpost the deliverables.** Say "Deliverable 1 of 4" through "Deliverable 4 of 4". An interrupted panel can then find its way back.
- **Close on the decision.** Thanking the panel is fine, but the last substantive words should be the ask.

### Voice: "we", not "I"

The role is a function that serves the CFO and the markets, not a personal platform.

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "I will drive alignment" | "The team brings the options; the CFO decides" |
| "I built a framework" | "We propose a framework, to agree with the CFO" |
| "My approach is…" | "The way this function works is…" |
| "I'll make sure the GMs…" | "The GMs own their numbers; we make them comparable" |
| "I believe strongly that…" | "The data points to…" / "Our recommendation is…" |

Keep "I" for two moments only: your personal stories, and "where I'd push back". Everywhere else, use "we", "the team" or "the function".

### Avoiding verbosity

- Each slide gets one point, two or three supporting lines, and a bridge to the next slide.
- If a sentence needs a breath in the middle, split it.
- Drop filler openers: "So basically", "What I want to highlight here is", "As you can see". Start with the point.
- Silence is fine. A two-second pause after a number reads as confidence.
- If you're asked something you've already covered, give the short answer and the slide number. Don't re-present it.

### Nubank's culture

Use these as a lens, not as slogans. Two of Nu's values are confirmed in its own wording:
- "think and act like owners";
- "pursue smart efficiency".

Nu's other values are commonly listed as:
- customers love us fanatically;
- challenge the status quo;
- strong and diverse teams.

Check the exact wording on Nu's careers page before quoting any of the other three.

How that shows up in delivery:

- **Customer first.** Tie spend back to the customer economics: cost to serve $1.0 against $17.1 of ARPAC; mature cohorts at about $31.
- **Smart efficiency.** The $35M is about 0.2 points of efficiency ratio. Say it plainly, and don't dramatize it.
- **Owners.** GMs own their cases and numbers. The function makes them comparable and keeps the rules public.
- **Challenge the status quo.** Be direct about what's broken: asks arrive as one number, with no shared format. Don't soften it.
- **Simplicity.** Use plain words and few meetings. The deck already says "nothing else goes on the calendar".
- **Test-and-learn.** Low-and-grow budgets, two hiring waves with gates, and quarterly keep / fix / drop reviews. Say "we'll change it if it doesn't work".

Don't attribute views to Rob Livingston or David Vélez that you haven't seen them state publicly.

## 1. Talk track, slide by slide

**Time:** 1.0 min. **Land this:** three problems at once; we treat finance and governance as growth levers.

> Good morning, and thank you for the time.

**Avoid:** reading the three cards. The panel can read them.

### Slide 2: Establishing principle-based structure for each deliverable

**Time:** 1.6 min. **Land this:** one principle, ownership, used four times; three decisions requested today.

> One principle runs through all four deliverables: ownership. We propose the method, gather the data and the viewpoints, and bring options with a recommendation. The decision, the reasons and any dissent are written down.
>
> For planning, we partner with Global Finance and FP&A to stand up a planning hub, with one business-case method and a funding schedule. For the Board, the book ships seven days before every meeting. For the HQ, four decision gates in a hundred and twenty days. And for how we work: inside the business and its numbers, with the CFO owning the decision.
>
> Success is measurable. One hundred percent of discretionary spend against an approved case. The Board book out seven days early, every quarter. HQ gates one and two cleared by mid-December. And the GMs rating the team twice a year as a growth partner, not bureaucracy.
>
> Three decisions today: the twenty-five million cap and its release schedule, the Board calendar, and the HQ gates.

**Avoid:** explaining each tile in depth; the next slides do that.
**Bridge:** "Deliverable 1 of 4: planning."

### Slide 3: A lean hub and three meetings to centralize planning

**Time:** 1.6 min. **Land this:** a small hub, built with Global Finance; the markets own their numbers.

> Deliverable 1 of 4: planning.
>
> We'd stand this up with Global Finance and FP&A, not alongside them. A hub of four to six people. The hub and the market finance teams report to Global Finance, with a dotted line to each country CFO.
>
> It's measured on three things: cycle time under forty days, forecast accuracy, and one hundred percent of discretionary spend with an approved case. AI agents handle the consolidation and the follow-ups, which is why the team stays small.
>
> Three meetings, and nothing else on the calendar. Bi-weekly stand-ups with local finance, monthly one-to-ones with country CFOs, and quarterly GM reviews with the CFO.
>
> The split matters most. Local finance owns the drivers and the assumptions. We standardize and consolidate. The CFO decides.
>
> The biggest risk is the markets not buying in. That's why the team sits in the markets, not only at the center.

**Avoid:** "my team". Say "the hub" or "the team".
**Bridge:** "Here's who that team is, and when we'd hire."

### Slide 4: Hub of 4-6: 2-3 in the countries, 2-3 central in the US, in two waves

**Time:** 1.2 min. **Land this:** hire half now and half only if the quality gates pass.

> The team, and how we'd build it. Two or three people in the countries, two or three central in the US with Global Finance.
>
> Wave one, by the second quarter of 2027: a global planning manager, and finance partners in Brazil and Mexico. Wave two comes by the fourth quarter, and only if the gates pass. That adds an analyst for data and the AI agents, a partner in Colombia, and a second analyst if volume requires it.
>
> The gates are simple. Cycle time under forty days on the first release. Every first-release dollar with a case. Forecast accuracy improving. Positive feedback from the GMs at mid-year. If a gate fails, wave two waits and we fix the gap first.
>
> The levels are a proposal. They map to Nu's leveling when we hire, and the cost counts against the same twenty-five million.

**Avoid:** defending each role. The gates are the point.
**Bridge:** "Now the money: the thirty-five million gap."

### Slide 5: Prioritized staged deployment > flat cuts across the board

**Time:** 1.8 min. **Land this:** $35M is small for Nu; the precedent is what matters, so budgets work like Nu's credit.

> First, scale. Thirty-five million is about fifteen basis points of annualized revenue. About two tenths of a point on the efficiency ratio. So this isn't about the money. It's about the precedent.
>
> The efficiency ratio moved from seventeen point six to nineteen point five percent between Q1 and Q2. Cost to serve is a dollar a month against seventeen dollars of ARPAC. That gap stays wide only if central costs grow slower than customers.
>
> What's broken today: asks arrive as one number, with no shared format to assess them. Whatever we reward this cycle is what we'll get next cycle.
>
> We looked at three options. An equal cut is fast, but it teaches everyone to pad. Core markets first is simple, but starves the new bets. We recommend staged release on business-case readiness.
>
> The principle comes from Nu itself. The 20-F describes low-and-grow: small credit limits, raised on behavior. Budgets can work the same way. Start small, and grow when activation, retention and unit cost hold.

**Avoid:** overselling the $35M. Say the small numbers calmly.
**Bridge:** "How a case gets scored and decided."

### Slide 6: Proposed investment and spend prioritization framework

**Time:** 1.6 min. **Land this:** mandatory spend has its own gate; discretionary asks compete on published criteria; clear owners at every step.

> Two kinds of spend, two paths. Mandatory obligations, like regulatory work, pass a separate gate. Discretionary asks compete for the envelope.
>
> Discretionary asks are scored on five criteria, with economic value weighted highest, at thirty percent. The weights are published before anyone submits, so the argument is about evidence, not the number. And the score informs the decision; it doesn't make it.
>
> Four steps, each with an owner. The business and local finance submit one case. Global Planning calibrates across markets. The CFO approves within the cap, and the CEO settles strategic trade-offs. Then owners report actual spend and benefits monthly, and we reallocate quarterly.
>
> The weights are a proposal, to agree with the CFO.

**Avoid:** walking through the scoring formula. Point at it.
**Bridge:** "And here's how the money flows through the year."

**Time:** 2.0 min. **Land this:** $25M is the cap, in two releases; the rest has a conditional path; every dollar of the $60M has an answer.

> Every dollar of the sixty million has a path. Read it from the bottom.

>

>

**Avoid:** saying the full $60M is "approved". Only $25M is in the plan; the rest is conditional.
**Bridge:** "That's the money. Deliverable 2 of 4: the C-suite and the Board." Pause here for questions.

## 4. Q&A for v10

Each answer is under 45 seconds. Start with the answer, keep "we" voice, and end on the slide number.

**Q1. Your plan has $60M of paths. Isn't that just approving the full ask?**
No. Only twenty-five million is in the plan, in two releases. The checkpoint opens only from profit above plan and inside the efficiency guide; if H1 doesn't beat plan, it's zero. The ten-million reserve needs MTeam validation. Slide 7.
*Tone:* calm; start with the "No".

**Q2. Why a $10M reserve on top of a $25M checkpoint? That looks like padding.**
They do different jobs. The checkpoint rewards units beating plan. The reserve covers the regulatory or strategic surprises every year brings, so they don't break the cap. Neither is in the plan, and both have a named decision owner. Slide 7.

**Q3. Who decides the 70/30 split?**
The CEO and CFO, at the lock sixteen days before the Board. We bring the options, both executives' positions and our recommendation. Ours is seventy-thirty, because mature cohorts earn about thirty-one dollars a month against seventeen for the group. Slides 7 and 9.

**Q4. Doesn't a planning hub create HQ versus the markets?**
It can, if it's built top-down. So the partners sit in the markets, local finance owns the numbers, and the GMs rate us twice a year. If that rating falls, we've become bureaucracy, and we'll know early. Slides 3 and 15.

**Q5. Why hire in two waves?**
Same logic as the budgets: start small and grow on evidence. Wave two happens only if the first release runs on time, every dollar has a case and the GMs see value. Otherwise we fix the gap before adding people. Slide 4.

**Q6. Does the hub eat into the $25M?**
Yes. Hiring cost is central opex and counts against the cap. The team is small by design, and the AI agents handle the consolidation work that usually takes headcount. Slide 4.

**Q7. How do you handle a senior GM who won't accept the result?**
Privately first, on what they're trying to achieve. Then on evidence: what would prove the ask works. Usually that leads to a funded start and a route to more. If they still disagree, the dissent is recorded and the CFO decides. Slide 10.

**Q8. What if the two executives still disagree at the lock?**
Both positions go in the memo, the CEO decides, and the dissent is recorded. The Board sees one recommendation and knows an alternative was considered. Slide 9.

**Q9. Why is the Board book late today, really?**
Our hypothesis: no single owner, no locked outline and no standard tables. We'd test that in the first week with the Corporate Secretary. The countdown fixes all three. Slide 9.

**Q10. Which HQ site would you pick?**
We wouldn't pick it ourselves. Four pass-or-fail tests come first, then five criteria. Two sites go to term sheet, and the CEO and CFO choose. We'd start where Nu already has licenses and a local CFO. Slide 11, backup J.

**Q11. Would you sign for the HQ entity?**
No. Licensed executives sign once they're approved and insured. The team runs the process, and the experts own the technical calls. Slide 12.

**Q12. Legal says no, Tax says yes. What happens?**
Both views go in the log, in their own words. If there's no joint view within forty-eight hours, it goes to the CFO with a recommendation. On legality, Legal's no stands. Slide 12.

**Q13. What's your walk-away on the grant?**
A cliff-edge clawback, or a headcount commitment above our plan. A smaller grant that scales is better than a bigger one that bites. Slide 13.

**Q14. Did AI write this?**
No. AI supported it, it didn't drive it. Claude, Gemini and Copilot helped with research, checking numbers, references, best practices, some formatting and testing assumptions. The ideas, the writing and the calls are mine. The agents we propose would run on whatever model Nubank approves. Backup K.
*Tone:* relaxed and direct. This is one of the "I" moments.

**Q15. Where would you push back on the CFO?**
When a decision skips the published rule. We'd raise it privately, with the numbers and the precedent it sets, and then back the call. Slide 14.
*Tone:* respectful and direct. Look at the CFO when you answer.

**Q16. What would you leave alone in the first 90 days?**
Local CFO decision rights, specialist governance like credit models, and the IR calendar. They work, and they belong to the people who run them. Slide 14.
